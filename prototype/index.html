Explain the 9.4

A chunk is lost only if all three of its nodes are among the 50 that failed. For a chunk on three random nodes that probability is the number of triples inside the failed set over the number of triples in the cluster:

C(50,3) ÷ C(5001,3) = 19,600 ÷ 20,833,332,500 ≈ 1 in 1,060,000

Ten million chunks each face that one-in-a-million chance, so on average 10,000,000 × 9.4×10−7 ≈ 9.4 are lost per outage. Under this model the expectation does not depend on how replicas are placed, because a uniformly random outage is equally likely to contain any particular triple. Correlated failures, such as a rack losing power, break that symmetry.

The count of lost chunks is close to a Poisson variable with mean 9.4, so the chance of losing none is e−9.4 ≈ 1 in 12,000. That is where the 99.99% comes from: not from any one chunk being fragile, but from there being ten million of them.

2 · The switch

Change nothing about replication. Only change which nodes share chunks.

The simplest copyset construction, used here to expose the effect: divide the 5,001 nodes into 1,667 fixed groups of three, and put every chunk on exactly one group. Same three copies, same storage, same nodes. (Production Copyset Replication overlaps groups so failed nodes recover faster; see the notes at the end.)

Copysets lose data far less often. When they do, they lose far more at once. Multiply the two and the expected chunks lost per outage comes out the same: 9.4.
Why exactly the same

In this model an outage kills a uniformly random set of nodes, so it is equally likely to take out any particular triple of nodes. A chunk is lost when its triple is taken out, and that chance is the same for every chunk no matter which triple it sits on. So the expected chunks lost per outage is fixed by the cluster size, the outage size and the chunk count alone. Placement decides only whether that expectation arrives as a trickle or as a flood.

This symmetry breaks under correlated failures, such as a rack losing power. There, placement does change the expectation, which is what failure-domain-aware placement is for.

Read the two charts. Random placement loses a little data in almost every outage. Copysets lose nothing in almost every outage, and when they do lose, they lose a whole group at once, about 6,000 chunks. The expected chunks lost per outage is the same, so the expected totals are the same. The copysets total takes far longer to settle on that expectation, because it arrives in rare lumps. A year of operations may not look like the mean at all.

Both clusters have the same expected chunks lost per outage. Which one would you rather run?

The paper's argument. When a data-loss incident carries a substantial fixed cost that does not scale with bytes lost (a page, an investigation, customer notices, a restore from backup), fewer and larger incidents are preferable to frequent small ones, because the fixed cost is paid per incident. Under that cost model the number to minimise is the probability of an incident, and copysets cut it by roughly 600× with no change in storage or replication.
Where it flips. If your cost is proportional to bytes lost, with no fixed cost per incident, the two clusters are equivalent and the argument evaporates. Expected chunks lost per outage is not the wrong number. It's the wrong number on its own. Reliability is a distribution, and you need to know which shape your cost function punishes.

What this leaves out, on purpose

  • Scatter width. Fixed groups of three are the extreme case. A dead node in such a group can only be rebuilt from two peers, which is slow. Real copyset designs let each node share groups with about ten others, trading a little incident probability for recovery speed. The shape of the result is the same.
  • The failure model. One percent of nodes chosen at random, dying together before anything can be re-replicated. Correlated failures within a rack or power domain are a different problem, handled by failure-domain-aware placement.
  • What is exact and what is sampled. Expected chunks lost per outage and the random-placement probability are closed forms. The copysets probability is computed by inclusion–exclusion over the 1,667 groups for exactly 50 failed nodes drawn without replacement (the page falls back to an independence approximation only in the optional explorer when the expected number of dead groups exceeds 25, where both round to 100%). The outages you watch are sampled from those exact distributions rather than from ten million stored placements, so the page stays small and fast.

The mechanism is from Copysets: Reducing the Frequency of Data Loss in Cloud Storage, Cidon, Rumble, Stutsman, Katti, Ousterhout and Rosenblum, USENIX ATC 2013. This page exists to make one consequence of that paper felt: two clusters with the same expected chunks lost per outage can differ hundreds of times in how often you lose anything.
